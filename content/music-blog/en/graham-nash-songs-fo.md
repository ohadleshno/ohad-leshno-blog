Toward the end of my military service, Yisrael and I decided to take a brothers' trip to the United States. We spent a week at my brother's place in Chicago and a week at his brother's place in New York. In Chicago we explored the city, visited grocery stores, ate at the Rainforest Cafe, and checked out furniture in hipster shops. One day we visited Wicker Park, a neighborhood known for record shops and vintage clothing stores. We stumbled into a store I remember fondly to this day, Dusty Groove, a gem for vinyl lovers. We met a clerk who used to be a PHP developer before switching to record sales because he grew tired of tech. He was an expensive brother who recommended various niche records. One recommendation was the album I am writing about today. Thanks to him I discovered the iconic album "Songs for Beginners" by Graham Nash. My initial association with "beginners" was Udemy courses with annoying narrators on Python, but this album is a short course on how to love and process unrequited love. It made me reflect on life, love, and music.

Graham Nash is best known for his membership in the supergroup Crosby, Stills, Nash & Young. "Songs for Beginners" is Nash's debut solo album released in 1971. The album offers a window into Nash's soul: an introspection during tumultuous times in his life. Nash's ability to convey complex messages in a straightforward manner makes him one of the finest songwriters. He expresses intricate emotions in simple words, evoking strong feelings in his audience.

The opening track is "Military Madness". Nash immediately addresses the sensory whirlpool caused by constant state wars. The track is simple and clear with beautiful guitar accompaniment. Nash avoids pretentious language to convey his message, speaking in a simple, touching tone that gives the song its strength. He is not trying to be a self-righteous moralist: he simply wants to convey a vital message about the destructive impact of war, believing people connect best with messages delivered simply and lightly.

Another core theme running through the album is his breakup with singer Joni Mitchell. Many tracks reference their relationship. We start with "Better Days", a form of self-therapy where Nash describes processing the breakup:

<blockquote dir="auto">
"When your love has moved away
You must face yourself and you must say
I remember better days"
</blockquote>

The song contains brilliant self-reproach lines such as:

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Though you're where you want to be, you're not where you belong"
</blockquote>

We then continue to "Simple Man", which serves as the album's highlight, presenting Nash's deep understanding that at his core he desires simple things: he wants to love simply. This is a basic human aspiration present even in the biggest snobs.

Nash wrote about this in his autobiography:

"I came up with those lines first and put them together using the idea that my approach to love and life in general is a simple one. I wasn’t the same kind of deep thinker that Joan was. Even though I’d spent years educating myself about photography, politics, beat poetry, art in general, and the world at large, when you scratch the surface I’m a pretty simple man. It’s most obvious in my music."

The album achieved both commercial and artistic success and is considered one of the defining records of the 1970s. Across almost every track, Nash seamlessly blends his folk rock style and tenor voice with emotional lyrics expressing his soul. He enables the listener to feel his emotions, creating a powerful experience. Open your heart to it.

Enjoy the music.

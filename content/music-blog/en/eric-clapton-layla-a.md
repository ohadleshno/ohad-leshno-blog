I remember a ride back from my military base: I hitched a ride with someone to where my car was parked. During that period my car was parked outside the base. These were five-minute drives, short rides in summer when the whole car was hot from sitting in the sun all day. You get into the car and immediately shout "turn on the AC", everyone sweating after a long day on base. On that short drive, Eric Clapton's "Change The World" played on the radio. The friendly driver looked at me and said: "I love this song, it's nice, who is it?" I mumbled that I thought it was Clapton but wasn't sure. Getting out of the car, I realized I had never truly explored Clapton's music in depth and thought I should give one of his albums a chance. I opened Apple Music, played the live album "One More Car, One More Rider", and was hooked: I listened to almost nothing else for an entire month.

I deliberated over which Clapton album to highlight. I even wrote a full post on his live album: there is something about live albums I love, and I consider it one of the best live albums ever made. However, live albums naturally consist of hits, so focusing solely on that misses a large part of Clapton. That is why we will talk about another album representing one of Clapton's most significant artistic works.

Before diving into "Layla and Other Assorted Love Songs", let's review the band lineup. The album was recorded by Derek and the Dominos, formed by Clapton after leaving Cream. George Harrison and Clapton were close friends who collaborated frequently, with Clapton famously playing on <a href="https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/eric-clapton-the-beatles-my-guitar-gently-weeps-isolated-guitar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-indigo-600 dark:text-indigo-400 underline hover:text-indigo-500 font-medium break-all">While My Guitar Gently Weeps</a> (<a href="https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/while-my-guitar-gently-weeps-story-behind-song/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-indigo-600 dark:text-indigo-400 underline hover:text-indigo-500 font-medium break-all">a fascinating backstory</a>). All band members worked with George Harrison on "All Things Must Pass", and the band emerged from those sessions. Although short-lived, the synergy was magnificent.

The album opens with "I Looked Away". Initially the tempo seems relaxed, but as the song progresses the intensity builds, reflecting the protagonist's emotional processing of a breakup.

<blockquote dir="auto">
"She took my hand
And tried to make me understand
That she would always be there
But I looked away
And she ran away from me today
I'm such a lonely man"
</blockquote>

The song concludes with a guitar solo launching us into the album's high energy. This structure of starting slow and escalating repeats across the album: in "Bell Bottom Blues" and blues covers like "Have You Ever Loved a Woman" and "It's Too Late", another track exploring lost love.

Guitar duels form a major highlight, especially on "Anyday". The track features a guitar duel between Clapton and Duane Allman (from The Allman Brothers Band). The guitars scream what Clapton's heart yearns for:

<blockquote dir="auto">
"And I know anyday, anyday I will see you smile, I will see you smile"
</blockquote>

Clapton communicates his soul through his instruments. The guitar here serves as another line of communication to listeners, expressing his deep longing for love.

The absolute pinnacles of the album for me are two tracks that evolved from studio jams. "Why Does Love Got to Be So Sad" bursts with energy from the first second: the guitar solo is extraordinary. The second track, "Keep On Growing", was originally a jam that almost got left off the album until Bobby Whitlock <a href="https://www.songfacts.com/facts/derek-the-dominos/keep-on-growing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-indigo-600 dark:text-indigo-400 underline hover:text-indigo-500 font-medium break-all">insisted it was too good to cut</a>, gifting us this phenomenal track.

Finally we must talk about "Layla", perhaps one of the most famous songs in rock history. The story behind it is about unrequited love. <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Layla_and_Majnun" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-indigo-600 dark:text-indigo-400 underline hover:text-indigo-500 font-medium break-all">Layla</a> is a character from a Persian poem by Nezami about tragic love, which Clapton used as inspiration. While working with George Harrison, Clapton fell in love with Harrison's wife, Pattie Boyd, and wrote the song for her, hoping the message would persuade Boyd to leave Harrison. Indeed Boyd eventually divorced George and married Clapton ("I would divorce too for a song like that"). But like many rock stars with volatile lifestyles, alcohol, and substance issues, they divorced a decade later.

So much has been written about this album woven with complex history, deep love, and magnificent music. The album blends relaxed blues, fierce guitar duels, and raw emotion toward life's loves as only Clapton can deliver.

Enjoy the music.

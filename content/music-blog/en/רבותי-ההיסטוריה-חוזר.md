Recently I feel as though we are trapped in a repeating cycle where everything unfolds over and over in the exact same way, and perhaps, without noticing, we are creating it ourselves. It feels as if we have returned to live in the 90s and early 2000s. Suddenly there is a nostalgic awakening towards TV shows like "Lost", and bands like "HaDorbanim", which disappeared from stages before I even learned to walk, are returning for an exciting comeback. Mediocre popular songs like "I'm Good" by David Guetta receive new life in revamped versions. Even the classic Israeli game "Piposh" received a remaster and re-release.

In a broader perspective, it seems that in other aspects as well we are recreating the past: the modern Palmach operated in Syria this year, we find ourselves entering another battle in Lebanon, and attacked by ballistic missiles originating from Iraq and Iran, except this time at least there is no fear of chemical weapons and scary gas masks. And most surprising of all: Oasis, who would have believed, is once again creating a global storm with ticket sales for their shows, even though I am not convinced that more than 2% of Britons actually tried to get a ticket.

There are moments when I look at all this and ask myself: what is actually happening here? Are we truly stuck in the past, or is this a basic human tendency, to paint everything in shades of nostalgia? Is this feeling so deep and ingrained in us? I do not have a clear answer, but before anything, I want to tell you a short story.

When I was in elementary school, I participated in a special program for gifted kids, where instead of sitting in class every Wednesday, I would arrive at a place where learning was done differently. There were no exams, no pressures, just enrichment for the sake of enrichment, a revolutionary concept that could cause children to learn out of genuine curiosity. Or as Mr. Rat described traditional teaching methods in <a href="https://dai.ly/x2qsl7l" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-indigo-600 dark:text-indigo-400 underline hover:text-indigo-500 font-medium break-all">(Arthur Season 16 Episode 193)</a>: "another rare childhood magic turned into a Pavlovian reinforcement system."

As a classic nerd, I loved math riddle classes, detective puzzles, physics of music, drawing, zoology, web development, robotics, and more. Suddenly, I had one day a week where I both enjoyed myself and learned something new. But like any framework, there were also less conventional classes. One of them involved watching the movie Vertical Limit (not exactly a masterpiece) in small segments, while discussing its moral dilemmas. It took us almost half a year to finish that movie, but between us, it was still better than a regular class at my elementary school.

But the lesson that stayed with me to this day was a class called "Land Through Songs". We learned Israeli history through Palmach songs, underground songs, and war songs. Bab el-Wad, for example, and the timeless hit "Gentlemen, History Repeats Itself" by Haim Hefer. That song, with its irony, was my first encounter with the question of free will and the circularity of history. As a child, I wondered if everything really repeats itself, and if so, what is the meaning of our choices within all of this?

Those thoughts continued to accompany me in the background while my life went on as usual. I played PlayStation, went out with friends, life flowed. But now, as I am older, it is hard not to feel that same sensation: that history truly repeats itself. So if history is repeating itself, maybe we should also bring back the good things that existed.

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Gentlemen, history repeats itself
Nothing was lost, nothing forgotten.
We will still remember, under lead rain
How the Palmach marched in Syria."
</blockquote>

This year marked 25 years since "Silent Engines" (Menoim Shketim), an album that continues to resonate not only because of its music but also because of the feelings it evokes, especially in a period when reality itself feels like an endless loop. Among all the hits this album gave us, "Dark Dream" (Halom Keheh) caught me in particular, precisely because it speaks to that feeling of living in a loop, a feeling that cannot be ignored today.

<div class="my-8 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl shadow-lg border border-slate-200 dark:border-slate-800 bg-black"><div class="relative w-full pb-[56.25%]"><iframe src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/PLI2bcnboLY" title="Assaf Amdursky - Silent Engines (1999)" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen class="absolute top-0 left-0 w-full h-full border-0"></iframe></div></div>

Assaf Amdursky shared that the song started as a protest song about money and work, but over time evolved into something deeper and more personal, connected to the tragedy of the late Inbal Perlman. The lyrics sketch a figure who seemingly lost all meaning, asking only for an end or a new beginning. Through the words and melody, a feeling of emptiness, guilt, and hopelessness merges with the figure of Inbal, whose recording became the beating heart of the song. Assaf recalls:

###### "When I wrote the song, it started as a protest song about money and work. Then friends told me that this style didn't really suit me. They said the melody was beautiful, but recommended I change the words, so I started cutting the lyrics and putting them back together, and as I saw how the song was building, I realized it was somewhat about Inbal's story, her death, and what she symbolized to me."

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Something inside
Died years ago
And the noise in the head
Not enough time to breathe
A lot of guilt feelings
A lot of conscience pangs
And a pit of emptiness."
...
"In the newspapers, echoing headlines
And the heart breaks
And everything remains
Pale as always."
</blockquote>

When I heard Inbal's voice, especially with Assaf's sampling that slowed down her speech, these feelings touched me deeply. Her simple and perhaps even childlike words, spoken while the country was still trying to digest Rabin's assassination, sound like a painful call against the absurdity of life. "We are all so cynical all the time," she said, "we open a newspaper and say oh well, another disaster, but it isn't like that." And indeed, something in those words sounds like a painful mirror on the human ability to normalize anything, even things that must never be normalized.

Perhaps here lies the core issue. It is possible that the loop we feel, the sense that history repeats itself, stems from the fact that we are constantly suppressing and normalizing. Sensation turns into apathy, and we lose the ability to dream of something different, of real change. But it isn't like that.

Or perhaps history does not repeat itself. Maybe we are simply living in a moment where there is no hope. I recently heard Etgar Keret say on a podcast that we live in an era where everyone feels they are losing. I am not sure he is right. It seems to me that we live in an era where no one believes anymore that anything can change. Once, even an artist like Aviv Geffen could take a song like Imagine and make a version demanding peace, something that today has become almost a taboo concept. Creating a protest song that truly sweeps people up, placing a mirror that says in the simplest words: what is the future? What do we want to happen, where is the hope?

<blockquote dir="auto">
We will conquer peace
And not the territories
Let's try
Until it gets better
Until it is
</blockquote>

But today? Instead of a demand for peace, we are repeatedly stuck in headlines, in fears, and above all, in the inability to dream of something else.

One of the bands I discovered this year and connected with deeply is The Diaspora Yeshiva Band. One of the prominent songs they perform is Dreams Come True, originally written by Abie Rotenberg. This song is touching, speaking of a Jewish person who dreams and does everything in his power to ensure freedom for his brothers under oppression, especially in the context of Jews who lived behind the Iron Curtain. It expresses the sense of responsibility and deep connection between Jews around the world, even those who have never met. This song, ending with a feeling of a dream fulfilled, reminds me how important it is to hold onto hope, even when reality seems not to permit it.

<blockquote dir="auto">
How I dreamed that one day we could be together,
Doing all the things that brothers do.
But I feared that I would have to dream forever,
For only dreamers still believe that dreams come true.
</blockquote>

So I will conclude, once again, with hope. With hope that all the hostages return home, and that we can feel with a complete heart that someone is truly doing everything so that happens, not just organizing ceremonies no one asked for and using songs without asking artists. With hope that this war ends quickly, and that we see better days ahead.

Because as Inbal said, "it isn't like that", but maybe, just maybe, it can be different.

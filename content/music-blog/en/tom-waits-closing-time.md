This week we celebrated Valentine's Day, February 14, and I wondered: why do we call it "Valentine"? After all, we don't have a day of anger (well, there is a day of anger, but a request not to get into petty things is fine). Why does love have a special day? I wondered what the origin of the holiday was, after in-depth research that included going to Wikipedia, writing in a blazer about "the 12 most valuable women in the 21st century" and at least watching 2 YouTube videos on how to start dating women, I will summarize the main points for you. Like everything in Western history, Valentine's Day began as a Christian commemoration of Saint Valentine, and in more modern times, the holiday has become in popular culture today a commemoration of romantic love. There are many theories as to how this happened, but I'm not a historian, and I think you should listen to an episode of the rest is history and you'll know more than some cynic on Facebook.

"It is said that one of these saints raised Christian Roman soldiers who were persecuted for their religion during the time of Emperor Claudius II Gothicus, and was subsequently executed. Before his death, he wrote a letter to a jailer's daughter, with whom he was friends, after she was cured of blindness, and signed "Yours, Valentinus". Over the years, Valentinus became the patron saint of lovers.

As couples around the world celebrate their love on this romantic holiday, it's important to remember that not every love story has a happy ending. For some, Valentine's Day is a painful reminder of lost love, broken relationships and broken hearts. Therefore, because I am an unusually cynical person and unable to uncritically contain the Tomariade that surrounds this day, I thought what would be the most appropriate album that I could write about that would not make me feel all over my body. Here comes Tom Waits, the troubadour with the gravelly voice, whose music speaks to the soul of the brokenhearted. His debut album, "Closing Time", is a masterpiece of melancholy and longing, perfectly suited for those navigating the turbulent waters of heartache. Before I start to expand on this wonderful album, I should mention that Gil Matos did an excellent program on the album, I much more recommend hearing it.

In the ever-changing landscape of music, there are albums that stand as monuments to the essence of an era, capturing its spirit in a way that transcends time. Tom Waits' debut album, "Closing Time", is one of these masterpieces. Released in 1973, it served as an exciting introduction to Waits' unique blend of jazz, blues and folk, and laid the foundation for his legendary career. Perhaps what makes "Closing Time" truly timeless is its universality. While the album is undoubtedly rooted in the Los Angeles music scene of the 1970s, its themes of love and loss are as relevant today as they were nearly five decades ago. In an age of constant change and uncertainty, there is something comforting about revisiting Waits' weary voice and knowing that even if the world changes a lot, some truths remain constant.

At its heart, "Closing Time" is a journey through the smoky, half-dark alleys of the human experience. From the bleak nostalgia of "Ol' 55" to the mournful ballads of "Martha", Waits concocts a tapestry of emotions that resonates with listeners on a deep and personal level. Each song feels like a frozen photograph of a moment in time, capturing the beauty and pain of life in all its being. In "Grapefruit Moon," Waits paints a vivid picture of lost love and the search for redemption. The melancholic melody and evocative lyrics depict the heartache and desire for a second chance. But despite the darkness, there is a glimmer of hope when the main character finds solace in the beauty of nature - a grapefruit moon hanging in the sky. And on the other hand in "Lonely" a soulful ballad explores the depths of loneliness and alienation. With its melancholy melody and stinging lyrics, the song captures the pain of feeling cut off from the world and missing human connection. However, in depicting loneliness, "Lonely" also offers empathy and understanding to people who are struggling. It's a reminder that loneliness is a universal experience, and even in our most difficult moments, we are never truly alone. At the same time, "Ice Cream Man" provides a departure from the more introspective pleasantries on the album, showcasing Waits' playful side. The song tells the story of an unusual ice cream seller who wanders the streets at night, offering sweet treats to people who need comfort. Although seemingly lighthearted, "Ice Cream Man" carries a deeper message about the importance of finding moments of joy and connection in the midst of hardship. It's a reminder that even in the darkest times, there are still reasons to smile and unexpected sweet moments to be found.

"Closing Time" isn't just about heartache; It is also about resilience. Despite the pain of lost love, the album reflects the human ability to persevere and overcome. Through the songs "Martha" and "I Hope That I Don't Fall in Love With You," Waits illustrates this resilience by diving into the complexities of love and longing. In "Martha", the hero turns to an old love, and turns the wheel back to their shared past. (I have already written about it extensively) It is a touching reminder that even in the face of lost love, the memories remain and shape who we are and the course of our future relationships. In the same way, "I Hope That I Don't Fall in Love With You" captures the vulnerability of opening one's heart to love despite the fear of being hurt. By acknowledging these feelings, Waits encourages the listener to embrace their vulnerability and find strength in their ability to love again.

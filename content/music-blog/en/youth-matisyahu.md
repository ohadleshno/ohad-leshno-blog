"Listen, if he took himself seriously, he would have been a superstar," my brother summed up Matisyahu's performance we attended a month ago. It was one of the best shows I've ever seen. I have never seen someone so natural on stage, so effortless and at the same time conquers your whole world and for two hours manages to produce a different spiritual experience. Of course, no human ear has yet been created that can understand even half of his reggae rap mutterings, but here and there among the dozens of messages he shoots into the air, he manages to shoot an arrow straight to the heart and open it wide. Even "One Day", such a clichéd and distant song, manages in the same situation to function like a sincere and strong prayer from the purest place. Matisyahu arrived in Israel during a difficult time and did a show whose proceeds went to the families of the abductees - an act that is very incomprehensible to me, especially after the journey he went through. Always when I think back to my Bar Mitzvah, I think this ceremony is super weird. Why should a 13-year-old boy do an event in the village of Maccabiah and invite 500 of his parents' friends? Personally, I did not feel that the age of 13 was the age when I became a "man" and received a "burden of Torah and Mitzvot". This whole event is very strange to me, but we'll put that aside. As part of the event, we invited a DJ and he asked me, as a bar mitzvah boy, what my musical style was. He brought me a list of about 2000 songs and asked me to choose. I chose about 50 songs, with the guideline being whether I had ever heard them. What 13-year-old really has musical taste that doesn't amount to Green Dee, at best? Because of the deep understanding that I do not understand, I went to consult with my sister. She said that at least 90% of the songs I chose were music that the human ear "can't contain" (in hindsight, I think 120% of them I wouldn't be willing to hear today). She didn't like the list he brought me at all and decided to create her own, while offering me all kinds of things here and there. One of the artists she suggested was Matisyahu. I automatically disqualified him outright because he is "Orthodox". Why do I need Hasidic music at my Bar Mitzvah? Even then I felt more connected to the Beatles than to Shawaki. I really disliked Hasidic music. She was perceived by me as inferior, because she represented an inferior culture. It's funny how the detachment from the traditional religious concept took place even then, in a very childish way.

Mathisyahu, originally Matthew Paul Miller, was born in Pennsylvania to a secular American Jewish family. In his teenage years, he decided for the first time to open up to Orthodox Judaism and began the process of repentance. Finally, towards the end of his studies at the New School, he decided to officially repent, at the same time as his schoolmate and future musical partner, the saxophonist Daniel Zamir, went through the process. After an unprecedented success, during which he became one of the only ultra-Orthodox artists to receive international recognition, Matisyahu went through a reverse process and decided to take a photo without the beard and announce his new way of leaving the religion. One of the most amazing aspects of Mathisyahu's music is his ability to cross cultural boundaries and unite listeners from all corners of society. Whether you're a devout follower or a Bob Marley-obsessed Rastafarian, there's something about Matthias' music that speaks to the soul—a universal message of hope, resilience, and spiritual awakening.

"Youth" is not only a rich blend of musical styles, but also a seamless fusion between two seemingly separate worlds: the lively rhythms of reggae culture and the deep spirituality of the Jewish Hasidic tradition. Through his music, Mathisyahu navigates between these worlds with the hand of an artist, giving each of them deep respect and awe, and creating a harmonious combination that speaks to the all-human human experience. Matisyahu harmoniously combines reggae rhythms with Jewish motifs, creating a soundscape that is both addicting and thought provoking. The juxtaposition of these elements is not intended to dilute any of the traditions, but rather to enhance and enrich them, and to emphasize the inherent similarity between them. "Fire of Heaven/Altar of Earth" takes us to a realm where spirituality blends with the raw energy of youth. There's an urgency in Matthias' voice, a passion that lights a fire inside you, urging you to embrace your identity with unabashed passion. And also in the following songs like "Jerusalem" I find a reminder of the rich cultural heritage that shaped me. Even if today she was taken to another place through him I am able to open up and look at her with different eyes and reconnect. And yet, between the pulsating rhythms and the enthusiastic service, there are moments of quiet contemplation. "What I'm Fighting For" and "WP" invite you to dive deep into the depths of the soul, to face the fears and doubts face to face. In these moments of vulnerability, I suddenly find strength - not in the answers but in the courage to ask the questions and face them.

One of the highlights of the album, "King Without a Crown", captures the essence of Matthias' live performances: the raw energy of the audience merges with the artist's passionate vocals. This is perhaps one of Matthias' most iconic songs, and for good reason. At its core, it is a moving anthem of self-discovery and empowerment. Mathisyahu's passionate vocals and penetrating lyrics touch deeply, urging listeners to embrace their inner strength and overcome difficulties. The song's catchy groove and uplifting message make it a timeless classic that continues to inspire audiences around the world.

The album "Youth" is more than an album - it is a testimony to the generative power of music to stir the spirit and ignite the soul. For me, it's a journey of re-discovery - a reminder to embrace the youth within and continue to bravely explore the depths of my identity and beliefs, all while embracing self-acceptance and immense love for a world I once felt was not mine.

Shlomi Shaban became a well-known artist in recent years, and this debut album contains some of his most famous hits such as "Arik", "Everyone Says", and "Puritan Whore" which most of you have likely heard. But since Shlomi specifically asks that we don't talk about "Arik", I will focus on the other songs that might be less known. The entire album captures the Tel Aviv experience of a confused bachelor, slightly chauvinistic and cynical. The album focuses heavily on relationships and self-image in the complex Tel Aviv landscape. Although released in 2000, the Tel Aviv atmosphere of that time remains alive today: some things never change. Route 5 bus is still route 5, and the concerns of the average Tel Aviver 20 years ago remain relevant today. Let's gradually board that Route 5 Tel Aviv line and stop at key stations along the way.

First stop: the eternal question: "Should I leave the country?". In "New York vs Yehezkel" we encounter character after character who decided to leave Israel, each with their own reasons: "everyone is up everyone's business", "verbal violence". Shlomi wonders whether he should also leave: "but maybe we really should travel away from here, maybe as always I think small".

Yet despite everything he ultimately stays: "because I am fine with this mediocrity". For those wondering, "Yehezkel was a completely nondescript spot at Metzitzim Beach where in 11th and 12th grade we held ironic singalongs before it became trendy," says Shlomi.

Second stop: the struggle against wanting to be "deep". Shlomi sings "I Wish I Was Deep", shouting his frustration to the world, wanting to be someone special who brings something not everyone can offer. It is a personal struggle with the pretentiousness he displays to the world versus an immense fear of impostor syndrome. Yet perhaps in some way, this genuine self-dialogue is what makes him deep in a certain sense.

Third stop: the desire not to be simple, expressed through a harsh perspective on simple people. In "An Ordinary Man" (clearly written about his father) he describes a banal and dull lifestyle of a person without character. The song serves as a warning sign for the kind of life you don't want to live.

In "Daniella", Shlomi describes a simple character representing the mainstream. She is so generic that radio stations decide what to broadcast based on her taste. Radio of course represents the voice of the simple crowd lacking unique taste.

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Daniella listens to what plays on the radio.
On the radio they play what Daniella listens to."
</blockquote>

Fourth stop: conflict with religion. Regarding the song "Tomorrow God Willing", Shlomi wrote: "This was the song I loved most on the album when it came out. The song that mattered most to me." The song is a prayer for a good new beginning. The contrast of the religious expression "God willing" spoken by a Tel Aviv guy who likely never stepped into a synagogue conveys desperation and a deep longing for someone to come save him. There is immense power in this contrast, heightened by the melody.

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Tomorrow God willing a new page opens for us, and everything that breathes will sweep me, take you, and we have nothing left except a name to call what moves us, our love."
</blockquote>

Final stop: cynicism and the ability to look at everything with a smile. Before releasing the album, Shlomi was relatively anonymous in popular music, focusing up to that point on classical piano. He performed with the Israel Philharmonic Orchestra under Zubin Mehta and Arie Vardi. After military service as an outstanding musician, he studied piano at the Royal College of Music in London, graduating with honors. After breaking through with "Arik", he recorded the album's final song "A Piece of Sky" (Hatechat Shamayim) with Shlomi Shabbat, a cover version of Leah Shabbat's song. Performing a song with someone famous when the only thing in common is a shared first name is brilliant wit. A piece of cynical genius that accompanies us throughout the album as its closing chord. <a href="https://youtube.com/clip/UgkxoUPuwyUcsy5VLjYBBAv4mbj8ejp45OhI" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-indigo-600 dark:text-indigo-400 underline hover:text-indigo-500 font-medium break-all">He even has a funny story about the name confusion on Carpool Karaoke</a>.

Shlomi manages to present Tel Aviv characters in an accessible way, making it easy to identify with their struggles and desires. The entire album is filled with wordplay and wit that blend harmoniously with his piano virtuosity. Shlomi himself is a cynical yet deeply sensitive figure. For someone who grew up and lives in Tel Aviv, he speaks as a voice for me as well. I will conclude with a quote from "Daniella", which while perhaps not the best song on the album, contains lines that belong in its pantheon:

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Everyone thinks about football in the middle. I think about a Grateful Dead song."
</blockquote>

Enjoy the music.

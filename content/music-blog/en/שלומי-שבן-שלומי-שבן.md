Shlomi Shaban has become a slightly more well-known artist in recent years, and this album has some of the most well-known hits, such as "Eric", "Everyone Says" and maybe even "Puritan Slut", which you have probably all heard. But, because Shlomi so requests that we not talk about "Erik", I will try to concentrate on the other songs that may be less well known. The whole album is simply the Tel Aviv existence of a confused single man, a bit chauvinistic and mixed. The album deals a lot with relationships and self-image in the complex world of Tel Aviv. The album may have been released in 2000, but the Tel Aviv existence of that time is as alive and kicking as it is today - some things never change. The Five Line is still the Five Line, phenomena that occupied the average Tel Avivian 20 years ago, are still present today. Let's slowly enter the Tel Aviv Line Five route, and pass through all the central stations.

The first stop: the eternal problem of the question - "Should we leave the country?". In "New York vs Ezekiel" we go through character after character who decided to leave the country, each one and their reasons: "everyone is in everyone's ass", "the verbal violence". Shlomi wonders to himself: maybe he too should leave the country "but maybe we really should leave here, maybe as always I'm thinking small".

And despite everything, he really stayed at the end - "because I'm fine with this misery". For those who were wondering, "Yehezkel was a completely casual place on Mitziz beach, in the 11th-12th grade we used to do a public poetry recital there in an ironic section before it became acceptable," says Shlomi.

Second stop: the struggle against being "deep". Shlomi sings "I wish I were deep", he shouts the frustration to the world, to be someone special, someone who brings something that not everyone can bring. Self-dealing with the feelings of the pretense he presents to the world, and in contrast the huge fear of impostor syndrome. But, maybe somewhere in this real self-talk, that's what makes it really deep in a way...

Third stop: the desire not to be simple with the super harsh treatment of people who are simple. In the song "A Normal Man" (which he clearly writes about his father) he describes a banal and boring lifestyle, of a person without character in life. The song is a kind of warning sign for what kind of life you don't want to lead.

And in the song "Daniela" Shlomi describes a simple character who represents the mainstream. She is so generic that the radio decides what to play according to her taste. The radio, of course, represents here the voice of the simple crowd without the unique flavor.

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Daniela hears what is being played on the radio.
On the radio they play what Daniela hears.'
</blockquote>

Fourth stop: the conflict with religion. About the song "Tomorrow with God's help", Shlomi writes: "This is the song I liked the most on the album when it came out. The song that was most important to me." The song is a kind of prayer for a new and good beginning. The contrast of the religious phrase "by God's help" to a Tel Avivian guy, who probably never set foot in a synagogue in his life, conveys the desperation and the deep desire for someone to come and save him. There is some very strong power in this contrast, and the melody of the song emphasizes it.

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Tomorrow, with God's help, a new page will open for us, and everything that breathes will sweep me away, take you away and we have no other place to read what we are moved by, our love"
</blockquote>

And the last stop: cynicism and the ability to look at everything with a smile. Shlomi, before the release of the album, was a fairly anonymous guy in the world of popular music, and up to this point was dealing in classical styles. He performed in the Philharmonic Orchestra conducted by Zubin Mehta and Aryeh Verdi. After the army, where he served as an outstanding musician, he went to London to study at the Royal College of Music in London, where he graduated with honors. After breaking through with the song "Erik" to the IDF's song parade, he recorded with Shlomi Shabat the last song on the album "Piece of Sky", which is a cover version on the album of Leah Shabat's vocals. To perform a song with someone famous, when the only thing they have in common is that their names are similar, is excellent wit. Just a piece of cynical genius, which accompanies us throughout the album, and this is its final chord. He even has a rather funny anecdote about The similar name in Carpool Karaoke.

Shlomi succeeds in presenting the Tel Aviv characters in an approachable way, so it is very easy to identify with their difficulties and desires. The entire album is full of puns and wit that blend harmoniously with his virtuosity on the piano. Shlomi himself is a cynical character but super sensitive at the same time. For a person who grew up and lives in Tel Aviv, he is a voice for me as well. To finish, I'll just put a quote from the song "Daniela", which may not be the best song on the album but with phrases that can enter the pantheon of this album.

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Everyone is thinking about football at the same time. I'm thinking of a Grateful Dead song. "
</blockquote>

pleasant listening

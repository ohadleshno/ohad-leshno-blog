I returned this week to Israel after my short journey in India. While I was there I could not stop thinking about how much the travel experience in developing countries has changed over the years. One element that changed drastically is the ability to stay in touch with the world. Even in the most remote and broken places I had reception and could make a WhatsApp call or post a blog entry for you with love. We live in a world where we post photos to Facebook as a summary of every activity, share every small experience in stories, and almost never live disconnect anymore. It is easy to know where a person is simply from their digital footprint on the internet, and that is only the tip of the iceberg: you can discover their political views and what kind of vegetable they love most. We live and are documented in ways that constantly connect us to the world.

I have nostalgic tendencies at times: most of the music I listen to was created before I was born. I own a record collection and try to listen to music on vinyl like some hipster. Generally I try to cherish the analog experience and find authentic elements in it that disappeared from the world. Something in instant culture prevents us from being in lack, being in absence, and thereby prevents us from yearning, experiencing, and even at times enjoying. During my stay in India I learned a bit about Buddhism and connected deeply to the following passage from the Tao Te Ching:

<blockquote dir="auto">
[2]
"All under heaven know beauty as beauty,
and thereby know ugliness;
all know good as good,
and thereby know evil.
Being and non-being create each other,
difficult and easy complement each other,
long and short define each other,
high and low incline to each other,
sound and resonance harmonize each other,
front and back follow each other.
Therefore the sage dwells in action through non-action,
and practices teaching without words."
</blockquote>

We begin to appreciate and understand things only when we do not have them, a phenomenon that operates with heightened intensity for me. Sometimes we must get lost in order to find ourselves, or as Henry David Thoreau said:

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Not till we are lost, in other words not till we have lost the world, do we begin to find ourselves, and realize where we are and the infinite extent of our relations."
</blockquote>

Therefore, with the theme of absence, we will talk about an experience that disappeared from the world yet remains intriguing today: the telephone operator in music. Even when I was young, directory assistance 144 still existed, a service where you could provide a person's details and they would try to trace their phone number. It still exists, but I am not sure who uses it when Facebook, Tinder, and similar platforms exist. So our playlist today is composed of songs around this connecting entity that gives us the ability to contact that lost connection once again.

Of course in most songs the sad idea being reached out to is a past love lost for one reason or another. We begin with Tom Waits in his wonderful song "Martha", where he calls his old love and tries to summarize forty years apart while dwelling on special moments they shared together.

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Operator, number please
It's been so many years
Will she remember my old voice
While I fight the tears?"
</blockquote>

A similar story exists in the song "Operator" by the Grateful Dead and "Operator" by <a href="https://www.ohadleshno.com/post/jim-corce-you-don-t-mess-around-with-jim" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-indigo-600 dark:text-indigo-400 underline hover:text-indigo-500 font-medium break-all">Jim Croce, whom I covered in a full blog post previously.</a> The protagonist of the song fails to reach the woman from the past; he tries to learn details about her but fails due to circumstances beyond his control. Finally he surrenders and arrives at the conclusion that he does not care about her status, as long as she is doing okay.

<blockquote dir="auto">
"She could be hangin' 'round the steel mill
Working in a house of blue lights
Riding a getaway bus out of Portland
Talking to the night
I don't know where she's going
I don't care where she's been
Long as she's been doin' it right"
</blockquote>

To add depth, I also included performances by Bob Dylan and Chuck Berry who also sing about long distance phone calls. They talk about the experience of connecting to someone far away and the journey undergone to make that call. I recommend diving into these songs yourself to experience a slightly different perspective on this element connecting us to distant worlds.

And of course, none other than Yehonatan Geffen, one of the greatest writers Israel produced, who passed away earlier this year, wrote an incredible song about that exact experience. In his album with Danny Litani "Letters to the Editor", an excellent album in every respect, we encounter the song "One Four". Yehonatan calls operator 144 and tries to locate someone whose number he had written on a cigarette pack that got erased. He has almost no details about her, but he desperately wants to apologize. We witness that experience described in Yehonatan's powerful words as only he could write. The accompaniment by Danny and wonderful arrangement by Miki Gavrielov and Haim Romano provide the somber atmosphere of the conversation.

<blockquote dir="auto">
"I just want to say I'm sorry,
and that I shouldn't have and I'll know for next time.
No I don't remember her last name,
but that's why you are there at one four.
(If the number requested appears in the phone book this call will cost one lira and twenty two agorot)
No she doesn't appear in the phone book,
she isn't the type to appear in books."
</blockquote>

The playlist concludes with ELO's grandiose performance of "Telephone Line", emphasizing the dramatic weight of waiting on the line for that pivotal moment. I wish all my readers a pleasant weekend: may we learn to miss, get lost, and find ourselves anew.

Enjoy the music.

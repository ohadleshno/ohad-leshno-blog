Towards the end of the mandatory service, Israel and I decided to take a brotherly trip to the Golden State (the United States for their part). We spent a week with my brother in Chicago and a week with his brother in New York. In Chicago we plowed the city, went to eat at the supermarket, visited Rain Forest café, and looked at furniture in hipster stores. One day we went to Wicker Park, a neighborhood known for its record stores and second-hand clothing stores. We came across a store that I remember very fondly to this day, Dusty groove, for fans of the genre. In the store we met a seller who was a PHP programmer in the past, who decided to convert to become a record seller, because he was tired of the field. He was an expensive brother, and he recommended us all kinds of niche records. One of the records he recommended to us was an album, which I'm going to talk about today. Thanks to the seller, I was exposed to the iconic album Songs for Beginners by Graham Nash. My first association with the word "beginners" has always been courses with annoying narrators on Udemy about Python, but this album is a kind of short course on how to love and deal with unrequited love. He made me think about life, love and music.

Graham Nash is best known for his membership in the supergroup "Crosby, Stills, Nash and Young". Songs for Beginners is Nash's debut album, released in 1971. The album is a look into Nash's soul; A kind of introspective about turbulent times in his life. Nash's ability to convey complex messages in a simple way is what makes him one of the best writers in the world. He is able to express complex feelings in simple words, and he manages to evoke strong emotions in his audience.

The first song on the album is Military Madness. Nash immediately begins to talk about the turmoil of the senses, which is caused to countries by the ceaseless wars. The song is simple and clear, with a beautiful guitar accompaniment. Nash does not use high or complex words to get his message across. He speaks in simple and touching language, which is what makes the song so powerful. Nash isn't trying to be an arrogant, self-righteous piece of shit. He simply wants to convey an important message about the devastating effects of war. He believes that people are more successful in connecting to messages that are conveyed in a simple and easy way, and that is what he does in this song.

The other issue that accompanies the album is his separation from the singer Joni Mitchell. Many of the songs on the album contain references to the relationship they had. We start with the song Better days, which is a kind of self-care. Nash describes his dealings with the breakup:

<blockquote dir="auto">
" When your love has moved away
You must face yourself and you must say
I remember better days "
</blockquote>

The song contains many ingenious self-reproach sentences such as:

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Though you're where you want to be, you're not where you belong"
</blockquote>

After that we continue with the song Used to be a king, a reference to Johnny's song I had a king, in which he describes the frustration of the breakup, and once again the future hope.

<blockquote dir="auto">
"Someone is going to take my heart But no one is going to break my heart again"
</blockquote>

The last song on the album, which deals with the subject of separation, is Simple Man. The song is the highlight of the album, and it shows Nash's deep understanding that, deep down, he wants simple things. He wants to love in a simple way. This is a basic aspiration that exists even in the biggest pussies.

Nash wrote about this in his autobiography:

"I came up with those lines first and put them together using the idea that my approach to love -and life, in general – is a simple one. I wasn’t the same kind of deep thinker that Joan was. Even though I’d spent years educating myself about photography, politics, beat poetry, art in general, and, well, the world at large, when you scratch the surface I’m a pretty simple man. It’s most obvious in my music."

The album was a commercial and artistic success, and is considered one of the most important albums of the 70s. In almost every song, Nash manages to combine in an amazing way the folk rock style and his tenor voice with emotional texts that express his soul. He manages to make the listener feel his emotions, and he manages to create a strong experience. Let your heart open.

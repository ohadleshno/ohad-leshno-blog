This week Eurovision began, an event that was once not just an occasion for people with a shared passion for pop music or generic tunes with zero musical sense. The concept of a music competition is inherently strange: from the start, how can music be measured on such a broad scale, and why is the competition categorized by country? Especially when today everyone simply sings in English with songs that could just as easily be generated by AI. But despite all my excessive cynicism, I still feel there is a kind of magic in it, a magic like musicals that return you to being a young child who wants to know how the story ends and for the hero to win. A childlike, innocent feeling with a touch of optimism and complete escapism. Today I want to focus on several points in Israeli culture from the contest, from those periods when we had self-respect and sang in Hebrew rather than in English with a poor accent. But before that, I must share a story.

There was a spot near the Wix offices at the Tel Aviv port where we ate lunch occasionally. The place was a kind of bland home-cooked food eatery that everyone named differently according to their seniority at the company. Once there was a hummus place with low hygiene that a pair of guys called "Diarrhea Hummus", and someone else called it Gabbay Falafel (take a guess why). Next to that spot was Izhar Cohen's jewelry shop. At the time, someone on my team had a partner at work and together they looked and acted like the old guys from the Muppets. On the way to one meal they explained to me a concept they called being "implicitly annoying", essentially doing annoying things but not in a direct way that would openly offend someone. A form of trolling that operates indirectly: for example, standing in the middle of an elevator instead of to the side when someone tries to enter. They were very proud of these jokes. Almost regularly on the way back to the office when everyone was sluggish from the heavy food, they would stand near Izhar's shop while he was inside and speak loudly to each other so Izhar would hear: "I can't believe Nissim Garame is inside Izhar Cohen's shop." Without a doubt they were living legends.

Izhar famously won Eurovision with the song "A-Ba-Ni-Bi", but he performed once again in Eurovision with the song "Ole Ole" in 1985. In those days people apparently liked dressing like Power Rangers and disco was at its peak. This pleasant Yemenite singer could have won once more if it weren't for the woman screaming at the top of her lungs while he sang "Ole Ole". <a href="https://www.youtube.com/clip/UgkxtV7qqCLXhuxe6GAgofDvEp3gv1a6GPFT" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-indigo-600 dark:text-indigo-400 underline hover:text-indigo-500 font-medium break-all">(minute 1:02)</a>

Fortunately her microphone flew off in the middle of the song, so he reached 5th place instead of 25th. By the way, he is the only male singer sent twice to Eurovision besides Ilanit, and he really resembles Nissim Garame (except he doesn't have falafel).

Another singer I love and discovered thanks to Eurovision is Avi Toledano, one of the most charismatic singers Israel has produced. He performed at Eurovision with the song "Hora", arranged by none other than Nancy Brandes. The song received "douze points" from Finland, which in retrospect turned out to be because the Finnish jury panel loved Toledano singing loudly "zona" ("hora" means whore in Swedish). The song "Hora" is another timeless classic, and like any classic, a microphone had to be kicked in the middle of his performance by one of the dancers to keep us from taking first place. <a href="https://youtube.com/clip/UgkxUoI84O154D71r_1cSRgObMvyQX3Zp0l7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-indigo-600 dark:text-indigo-400 underline hover:text-indigo-500 font-medium break-all">(minute 2:02)</a>

What I truly love about the song is Toledano's endless class: without pyrotechnics, without shiny screens, he simply manages to conquer the screen in a way no current Eurovision artist comes close to.

Many know that "Diva" was Israel's third victory at Eurovision, written by Svika Pick and performed by Dana International. But not many know that Svika is also the artist who appeared in the highest number of Kdam-Eurovision national finals. Pick participated no less than seven times, though he did not win any of them. One of his coolest and most unique songs was "Romantica", whose performance was heavily inspired by Sergeant Pepper with him dressed in royal military uniforms.

Another excellent band that performed at Eurovision is Kaveret, which performed in 1974 with the song "Natati La Khayay" (I Gave Her My Life). Contest regulations at the time allowed only six band members on stage, but Kaveret had seven members, so Yoni Rechter had to step in as the band's conductor. The song itself reached 7th place, while 1st place went to ABBA's "Waterloo". It is crazy to think that songs from that era remain beloved classics, unlike recent songs that disappear within a minute.

But the funniest thing that ever happened was sending the band Ping Pong to Eurovision in 2000, making it look as though the State of Israel gave up on the Zionist enterprise at that point. It is not only unbelievable how lacking in self-awareness and vocal ability they were, it is even more impressive that the Broadcasting Authority allowed them to reach the competition. They sang out of tune, and at some point began waving Syrian flags for completely unclear reasons, alongside lyrics that were provocative and pointless. It was top-tier low-grade trolling, just the way I like it. At one point the Broadcasting Authority realized what it did and wanted to sue them for a financial refund, but it was too late after multiple Syrian flag waves. By the way, the fake tattoo on the guy translates to table tennis.

For me, Eurovision serves as a reminder that not everything needs to be taken so seriously, that we can simply enjoy fun music, and that the story is more important than the performance itself. Wishing Israel much success, and hoping for better news soon!
